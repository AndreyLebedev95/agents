## The timetable

A one-hour session:

| Block | Time | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Welcome | 4 min | Explain how the test will work, so they know what to expect |
| Questions | 2 min | A few questions about them — puts them at ease, tells you how computer- and web-savvy they are |
| Home page tour | 3 min | Open the Home page; "look around and tell me what you make of it" |
| Tasks | 35 min | The heart of the test |
| Probing | 5 min | Your questions about what happened, plus any from the observation room |
| Wrapping up | 5 min | Thank them, pay them, show them to the door |

The Home page tour does double duty: it tells you how understandable the Home page is, *and* how much of
your domain the participant already knows — which you'll need when grading their difficulties on a curve.

## The introduction

Four things it must establish, in the participant's mind, before anything else happens:

1. **We're testing the site, not you.** You can't do anything wrong here. This is probably the one place
   today where you don't have to worry about making mistakes.
2. **We want to hear exactly what you think.** Don't worry about hurting our feelings — we want to improve
   it, so we need to know honestly what you think.
3. **Please think out loud** as we go: tell me what's going through your mind. This is what helps us.
4. **Here's how long this takes and what we'll do.**

reads this aloud from a printed page and says so: *"Before we begin, I have some information for you,
and I'm going to read it to make sure I cover everything."* Reading rather than improvising is not
awkwardness — it guarantees nothing gets dropped and signals that this is a procedure, not a judgment.

The reason this block matters so much: people who believe they're being evaluated behave differently and
blame themselves for the site's failures. That's also why they're called **participants**, never "test
subjects".

## During the tasks

**Your one job is keeping them thinking out loud.**

When they go quiet:

- "What are you thinking?"
- "What are you looking at?"
- "What are you doing now?"

**Influence nothing.**

- No leading questions.
- No clues.
- No assistance — unless they're **hopelessly stuck or extremely frustrated**. That threshold is
  deliberately high. Merely struggling is not stuck; struggle is the data you came for.
- When they ask for help: *"What would you do if I wasn't here?"*

**Save every probing question for the end block.** Asking why during the tasks biases everything that
follows. In the probing block you can ask about anything that happened, including questions the observation
room wants asked. Example: the team believed everyone would at least look at the five numbered step
illustrations at the top of the page, so he asked about them afterwards — and learned the participant
noticed them but didn't engage, because she wasn't ready to start the process and just wanted to look
around first. That's the kind of answer you only get by waiting.

**Three stopping conditions** for any task — end it when:

1. They finish it, or
2. They get really frustrated, or
3. **You're not learning anything new** from watching them muddle through.

The third one is what facilitators forget. Continued struggle producing no new information is just spent
session time.

## Writing the tasks

1. Start from a list of what people need to be able to do with the thing you're testing. For a login
   prototype, that might be: create an account; log in with an existing username and password; retrieve a
   forgotten password; retrieve a forgotten username; change the answer to a security question.
2. Choose enough to fill about **35 minutes**, remembering some people finish faster than expected.
3. Word each one carefully so it's unambiguous what you want them to do.
4. **Include anything they'd need but wouldn't have.** Credentials, account numbers, a scenario.
5. **Let them choose some details.**

On that last point, the difference is larger than it looks:

- Weaker: *"Find a cookbook for under $14."*
- Better: *"Find a book you want to buy, or a book you bought recently."*

The second increases their emotional investment and lets them use their own knowledge of the content, which
produces more revealing behaviour.

**Worked example of a fully specified task**, showing the embedded information:

> You have an existing account with the username `delphi21` and the password
> `correcthorsebatterystaple`. You've always used the same answers to security questions on every site, and
> you just read that this is a bad idea. Change your answer for this account.

Note what it does: gives the credentials they couldn't know, supplies a motivation so the action makes
sense, and states the goal without naming any screen or control.

**If all you have is a rough sketch**, the task is simply: *look at this and tell me what you think it is.*
That's a legitimate test and worth running.

## What you'll typically see

Three problem types recur, and naming which one you're watching makes the debrief faster:

- **Unclear on the concept.** They look at it and don't know what to make of it — or think they do, and are
  wrong.
- **The words they're looking for aren't there.** Either you failed to anticipate what they'd look for, or
  you're using your words rather than theirs. Example: a participant choosing "Fixed-price" when
  "RFP" was the option that would actually get her what she wanted — the kind of misunderstanding that
  surprises the people who built the thing.
- **There's too much going on.** What they want is right there and they don't see it.
