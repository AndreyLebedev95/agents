## Ground 1 — your own unmet needs

The first place to look is the mirror. Building something you use and believe improves lives is not only the defensible position, it is the one with the highest odds of producing something people want — because the designer always has a direct line to at least one real user.

The reframe that does the work:

> Instead of asking "what problem should I solve?", ask **"what problem do I wish someone else would solve for me?"**

What to look for in your own behavior:

- **Workarounds you built.** The notepad, the spreadsheet, the manual step you repeat, the reminder you set because a tool does not.
- **Over-specification.** Places an existing product forces you to specify more than you care about. One scheduling tool was born because every available client demanded an exact date and time, when all the founder wanted was to post five times a day, spread out — so he tracked it on paper until the paper became unbearable, and then built the thing that removed the specification.
- **Discrepancies between what a product offers and what you need it to do.** Not missing features — mismatched shape.
- **Steps you would remove.** Identify where steps could come out of the products you already use.

The daily practice: as you go about your day, ask yourself why you do or do not do certain things, and how those tasks could be made easier or more rewarding.

Careful introspection also informs breakthrough *improvements* to existing solutions, not only new products.

## Ground 2 — nascent behaviors

Behaviors that start in a small group can expand to a wide population — but only if they serve a broad need. The trap is that the small initial population reliably deceives observers into dismissing the product's true potential.

A partial list of things dismissed as toys or niche:

- A $1 preloaded roll-film camera, marketed as a child's toy and written off by established studio photographers.
- The telephone: *"The Americans have need of the telephone, but we do not. We have plenty of messenger boys."*
- Aircraft, in 1911: *"interesting toys but of no military value."*
- Data processing, in 1957: *"a fad that won't last out the year."*
- Reading books and newspapers online, in 1995: *"Uh, sure."*

When technologies are new, people are skeptical, and few have the foresight to see how an innovation will change their routines. Old habits die hard on the observer's side too.

**What to do with this:**

1. Find a behavior a small group already performs enthusiastically, without being told to.
2. Ask whether the need underneath it is narrow or fundamental. This is the discriminating question — a genuinely niche need stays niche no matter how well you sequence the rollout.
3. Expand along **adjacent, tightly connected populations** rather than launching broadly.

The canonical rollout: a social network that mimicked an existing offline campus ritual, opened at one school, then to similar schools, then to students nationally, then to secondary schools, then to employees of selected companies, and only then to everyone. What began as a nascent behavior on one campus served a fundamental need for connection.

Note the connection to positioning: many habit-forming technologies begin as vitamins — nice-to-haves — and become painkillers only after the habit relieves an itch that did not previously exist. That so many breakthrough companies were first dismissed as toys or niche markets is the same phenomenon seen from outside.

## Ground 3 — enabling technology

Technology waves follow a three-phase pattern:

1. **Infrastructure.** The preliminary advances that let a large wave gather.
2. **Enabling technologies and platforms.** These create the basis for new kinds of applications and take the wave to mass adoption.
3. **Applications.** Which crest, subside, and make way for the next gathering wave.

The internet's own sequence: government-commissioned infrastructure → dial-up and then broadband access → markup, browsers, and search engines at the application layer. At each stage, the previous enabling technologies let new behaviors and businesses flourish.

**Wherever new technology suddenly makes a behavior easier, new possibilities are born.** The creation of new infrastructure often opens unforeseen ways to make other actions simpler or more rewarding.

The specific question to ask: *where does this shift make cycling through an engagement loop faster, more frequent, or more rewarding?* That is fertile ground — not "what can this technology do", which produces demos.

## Ground 4 — interface change

Sometimes no technology change is required at all. Many companies have found new habit formation simply by identifying how changing the way people *interact* creates new routines.

Whenever a large change occurs in how people interact with technology, expect opportunities. Changes in interface suddenly make all sorts of behaviors easier — and when the effort required to accomplish an action decreases, usage tends to explode.

The pattern across decades: clunky terminals became graphical interfaces accessible to mainstream consumers; a cluttered, ad-heavy search experience became a stark one; social interaction online was simplified into new interfaces built on behavioral insight. In each case a new interface made an action easier **and uncovered surprising truths about user behavior** that were invisible before.

Two more recent instances worth the pattern rather than the specifics: a rich image canvas revealed how compelling an endlessly browsable visual catalog is; phone-integrated cameras plus simple filters made mediocre photos look good, and suddenly taking a good picture with a phone was easy. In both, tiny teams generated enormous value not by cracking hard technical challenges but by solving common interaction problems.

The forward-looking version of this ground: **live in the future.** Anticipate where interfaces are about to change and what behaviors that will make easy. New input modalities, new form factors, new ambient surfaces — each opens a window, and each also threatens an existing product whose advantage was the old interface.

## The field exercise

Run this when the candidate list is thin, or as a regular practice to train the eye.

**Week-long self-observation.** Be aware of your behaviors and emotions as you use everyday products. For each use, ask:

- What triggered me to use this?
- Was I prompted externally, or did it come from inside?
- Am I using this product as its designers intended?
- How could this product improve its onboarding, reengage users with additional external triggers, or encourage users to invest in the service?

**Three strangers.** Speak with three people **outside your social circle** — the outside part matters, since your circle shares your assumptions — and find out which apps occupy the first screen of their phones. Then ask them to use those apps as they normally would and watch. You are looking for:

- **Unnecessary behaviors** — steps they perform that nothing requires.
- **Nascent behaviors** — things they do that nobody designed for, workarounds they have normalized.

**Five interfaces.** Brainstorm five new interfaces that could introduce opportunities *or threats* to your business. The threat half is the one teams skip, and it is where an incumbent's advantage evaporates.
