# Formatting text for scanning

Read when formatting a body of text rather than a single label.

## Why formatting is doing real work

Much of the time — perhaps most of the time — people spend on a page is spent **scanning the text in search
of something**. They're looking for words and phrases matching (a) the task at hand, (b) their standing
personal interests, and (c) trigger words hardwired into everyone: *Free*, *Sale*, *Sex*, and their own name.
Everything else isn't read; it isn't even seen.

image for this is the old cartoon about what dogs hear: "blah blah GINGER blah blah blah blah
GINGER blah blah blah." What we see when we look at a page depends on what we have in mind, and it's usually
a fraction of what's there.

Formatting is what determines whether the fraction they catch is the fraction that matters.

Three reasons people scan rather than read, worth knowing because they're not going to stop: they're usually
on a mission and don't have time to read more than necessary; they know they only care about a fraction of
what's on the page; and they're good at it — scanning is a skill everyone learns alongside reading and has
been practising on newspapers and magazines their whole lives.

## Headings

Well-written, thoughtful headings interspersed in the text act as an informal outline or table of contents.
They tell people what each section is about — or, if they're less literal, they intrigue. Either way they let
someone decide which parts to read, scan, or skip.

**Use more headings than you'd think, and put more time into writing them.** This is the single highest-yield
formatting move, and the one most often under-done.

Two styling rules people overlook:

**Distinguish the levels unmistakably.** If you're using more than one level of heading, there must be an
obvious, impossible-to-miss visual distinction between them — each higher level larger, or with more space
above it.

```
Bad                                  Better
─────────────────────────            ─────────────────────────
Section heading                      Section heading      ← larger
Subheading                                                ← more space above
text text text text                    Subheading         ← visibly smaller
                                     text text text text
```

**Never let a heading float.** A heading must be **closer to the section it introduces than to the section it
follows**. This makes a large difference for the effort, which is why it also appears in the short
list of absolute rules at the end of the book.

```
Bad                                  Better
─────────────────────────            ─────────────────────────
...end of previous section           ...end of previous section

Heading

text of the new section              Heading
                                     text of the new section
```

Tell: equal or greater whitespace *below* the heading than above it. The reader assigns the heading to the
wrong section, which is a small cost paid on every heading on the page.

## Paragraphs

**Keep them short.** Long paragraphs present a **wall of words**:
daunting, harder to keep your place in, harder to scan than a series of shorter paragraphs.

You may have been taught that every paragraph needs a topic sentence, detail sentences, and a conclusion.
**Reading online is different.** Even single-sentence paragraphs are fine.

Practical method: examine any long paragraph and you will almost always find a reasonable place to break it
in two. Get in the habit of doing it.

## Bulleted lists

**Almost anything that can be a bulleted list probably should be.**

The mechanical way to find candidates: scan your paragraphs for any **series of items separated by commas or
semicolons**. Those are your lists.

For optimal readability, put a small amount of additional space between the items — a run-together list is
harder to scan than a paragraph.

```
Bad
Our plan covers medical, dental, vision, prescription drugs, mental health services, and
physical therapy.

Better
Our plan covers:

- Medical
- Dental
- Vision
- Prescription drugs
- Mental health services
- Physical therapy
```

## Highlighting key terms

Much page scanning consists of looking for key words and phrases. Formatting the most important ones in
**bold where they first appear** makes them easier to find.

Two constraints:

- If they're already text links, you don't need to — the link formatting already marks them.
- **Don't highlight too many things**, or the technique loses its effectiveness entirely. Bold everything and
  you've bolded nothing.

## Front-loading, for eyes and for ears

Screen-reader users **scan with their ears**. They're just as impatient as sighted users, want the
information as fast as possible, and don't listen to every word — they listen to just enough to decide
whether to keep listening, many at an astonishingly rapid speech rate.

The mechanism that matters for formatting: they hear **the first few words** of a link, line, heading, or
paragraph, and if it doesn't seem relevant they move to the next link, next line, next heading, next
paragraph. Where a sighted user might find a keyword by scanning over the whole page, a screen-reader user
**may never hear that keyword if it isn't at the beginning**.

So: put the distinguishing word first. In link text, in headings, and at the start of lines. It costs
nothing, it helps everyone scanning at speed, and it's the difference between findable and invisible for
anyone listening.

## Checklist

- [ ] More headings than felt natural, each one written rather than labelled
- [ ] Heading levels visually unmistakable from one another
- [ ] No heading floats — more space above each than below
- [ ] No paragraph longer than it needs to be; single-sentence paragraphs allowed
- [ ] Every comma- or semicolon-separated series converted to a list
- [ ] Extra space between list items
- [ ] Key terms bolded at first appearance only, and sparingly
- [ ] Distinguishing word first in every link, heading, and line
