## Where the "what is this" message lives

Three places on a page carry an explicit statement of what the thing is:

- **The tagline** — the space right next to the Site ID, one of the most valuable pieces of real estate on
  the page. A phrase visually connected to the ID is read as describing the whole site.
- **The welcome blurb** — a terse description in a prominent block, usually top-left or centre of the
  content area, so it's the first thing the eye catches.
- **The "learn more"** — a short explanatory video, for propositions novel enough to need more explanation
  than most people will read.

Most people try to guess what the site is from the overall content first. These exist so that **when guessing
fails, there's somewhere to find out.**

## Taglines

A tagline is a pithy phrase characterizing the whole enterprise — summing up what it is and what makes it
great. The form is old: *"Thousands of cars at impossibly low prices"*, *"More stars than there are in the
heavens"*, *"All the News That's Fit to Print"*.

They're efficient because a tagline sits in the one place where people most expect to find a concise
statement of purpose.

### What to aim for

| Attribute | Test |
|---|---|
| **Clear and informative** | Does it explain exactly what this organization does? |
| **Just long enough** | Six to eight words — enough for a full thought, short enough to absorb at a glance |
| **Differentiating, with a clear benefit** | The substitution test: could **nobody else in the world** use this except you? |
| **Personable, lively, sometimes clever** | Clever only counts when the cleverness *conveys* the benefit rather than obscuring it |
| **Not generic** | If it could sit on a competitor's page unchanged, it has failed |

Apply the substitution test literally. It is the fastest way to tell a real tagline from a pleasant noise.

### Tagline vs. motto

A **motto** expresses a guiding principle, a goal, or an ideal: *"We bring good things to life"*, *"You're in
good hands"*, *"To protect and to serve"*. Lofty and reassuring — and if someone doesn't know what the thing
*is*, a motto tells them nothing.

A **tagline** conveys a value proposition.

Confusing the two is the most common failure. When you're handed a motto and asked to fix the tagline, name
the substitution rather than polishing the motto.

One legitimate exception: an organization with a captive audience — a public utility, say — can get away with
a motto, because differentiation isn't its problem.

### Can a site skip it?

Some can: the handful with genuine household-word status, and sites very well known from their offline
origins. The argument is even those benefit:

- No matter how well known you are, why pass up an unobtrusive chance to tell people why they're better off
  here?
- A strong offline brand's **online mission is never exactly the same** as its offline one, and that
  difference is worth explaining.

## Welcome blurbs

A terse description of the site in a prominent block. Guidelines:

**Use as much space as necessary.** The temptation is to use none, for two reasons: you can't imagine anyone
not knowing what this site is, and everyone else is competing for the space. A genuinely novel proposition
earns a lot of the page — Kickstarter uses almost every element on its Home page to explain or reinforce what
it does, with "What is Kickstarter?" as the most prominent item in its primary navigation.

**And no more than necessary.** For most sites the basic proposition needs little space, and a message
occupying the whole page is usually too much for anyone to bother absorbing. Just long enough to make the
point, no longer. Don't feel obliged to mention every great feature — a few of the most important ones.

**Never use a mission statement.** Many sites fill the space with corporate mission statements that sound
like they were written by a pageant finalist: *"XYZCorp offers world-class solutions in the burgeoning field
of blah blah blah…"* Nobody reads them.

**Test it on outsiders.** This is one of the most important things to test, because you cannot trust your own
judgment: the main point is the one thing nobody inside the organization will notice is missing.

## FAQs

Frequently Asked Questions lists are enormously valuable — when they're real. Three conditions:

**They really are FAQs.** Not marketing pitches masquerading as questions. The counterfeit is
**QWWPWA — Questions We Wish People Would Ask.** These deplete goodwill rather than building it, because the
reader came with a real question and found an advertisement.

**They're kept up to date.** Customer Service and Technical Support can easily give you this week's five most
frequently asked questions. Recommended: put that list at the **top of the Support page**, always.

**They're candid.** Often people are looking in the FAQs for the answer to a question you'd rather they
hadn't asked. Candor in exactly those situations goes a long way toward increasing goodwill — and evasion in
exactly those situations is what people are checking for.

### Writing an FAQ answer

- Answer the question that was asked, in the first sentence.
- Use the asker's words, not the organization's. If people call it a "refund" and you call it a "return
  authorization", the entry is unfindable.
- If the answer is unfavourable, say it plainly and then say what someone can do about it. Being upfront
  about shipping costs, parking fees, or outages loses a few points and usually gains more for candor.
- If you genuinely can't do what they want, say that you know you're inconveniencing them. When in doubt,
  apologize.
