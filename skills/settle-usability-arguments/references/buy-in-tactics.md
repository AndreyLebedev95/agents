## Ordered by cost

### 1. Get someone senior to watch one session — in person

The tactic Arguably works best, and by a wide margin the cheapest.

**The ask:** tell them you'll be doing some testing and it would be great for the team's morale if they could
poke their head in for a few minutes. Low commitment, easy to say yes to.

**What happens:** executives often become fascinated and stay longer than they planned — because it's the
first time they've seen anyone actually try to use the company's site, and the picture is usually not as
pretty as they'd imagined.

**Why in person is non-negotiable:** the difference between watching a test live and hearing a presentation
about it is the difference between watching a sporting event as it happens and hearing the recap on the
evening news. Live games create memorable experiences. Recaps don't.

This is why observing is more generally valuable than it looks: for many people it's a transformative
experience that changes how they think about users — they suddenly get that users aren't like them. That
conversion is what you're buying, and it doesn't happen from a document.

### 2. If they won't come, use video

In descending order of effect:

- Clips of test highlights inside a presentation you're already giving.
- If you don't get to present: a clip **under three minutes** on the intranet, with an intriguing description
  and a link emailed round. Even executives watch short videos.

### 3. Do the first test without asking

Don't ask permission. Keep it incredibly simple and informal, find volunteers for participants so it costs
nothing, and do it on your own time.

Then make sure **something improves as a result**:

- Pick an easy target — something you already know has at least one serious usability problem that can be
  fixed quickly, without needing a lot of people to sign off. Renaming a poorly labelled button is the
  archetype.
- Test it, fix it, **publicize it**.
- If there's a simple way to measure the improvement, use it. Test something that's been generating a lot of
  support calls, then show how much calls on that issue dropped after the fix.

The logic: you're not arguing that testing works, you're producing an artifact that already did.

### 4. Test the competition as a public event

Testing competitors' sites is worth doing at the start of any project anyway — you learn what works without
building anything. It's also a great way to drum up support: everybody loves learning about the competition,
and because it isn't *your* site being tested, **nobody has anything personally on the line**. It makes a
good brown-bag lunch event.

Use this when the political temperature is too high for anyone to volunteer their own work.

### 5. Present findings live, not as a report

Written what he called the "big honking report" for years before concluding it wasn't worth the time and
effort. A live presentation lets people ask questions and voice concerns — something a written report can't
do. And teams doing Agile or Lean development have no time for written reports anyway.

Reserve writing for the short list of agreed fixes with owners.

### 6. ROI and business-speak — real, but expensive

The two suggestions Note he's always heard:

**Demonstrate ROI.** Gather and analyse data proving a usability change produced cost savings or additional
revenue — "changing the label on this button increased sales by 0.25%". The reference work is Bias and
Mayhew, *Cost-justifying Usability*.

**Speak their language.** Instead of talking about benefits for users, learn the current vexing corporate
problems and describe your work as part of the solution — pain points, touch points, KPIs, whatever
management vocabulary is trending locally.

Both are fine ideas and worth doing if you can manage it. The caveats are real though:

- An ROI case tied to costs and revenues is **a lot of work**, and unless it's rigorously implemented there
  will always be someone who claims the added value came from something else. You can win the analysis and
  lose the argument.
- Learning to speak business fluently is genuinely challenging. As puts it, that's what MBAs are for.

Which is why these sit at the bottom of the list rather than the top. They're what you do once you already
have a foothold, not how you get one.

## Posture

Two things that determine whether any of the above lands.

**Empathize with management, for real.** observation from a UXPA conference: what a nice group of
people — and then, of course they are, empathy is virtually a professional requirement for usability work. If
you're drawn to this, you're probably empathetic. Apply it to your bosses. Not in the "how can I figure out
what motivates these people so I can get them to do what I want" way, but in the "understand the position
they find themselves in" way, with real emotional empathy for them. The effect is often surprising.

**Know your place in the grand scheme of things.** A little humility goes a long way. The reality is that in
the business world almost everyone is a very small cog in a huge collection of cogs. You want your enthusiasm
to be infectious, but it does not work to go around with the attitude that you're bringing the truth — about
usability or anything else — to the unwashed masses.

> Your primary role should be to share what you know, not to tell people how things should be done.

## Knowing the territory

Useful background when someone waves a title at you.

In the late 1990s, **Usability** (making sure things can be used successfully) and **Information
Architecture** (making sure content is organized so people can find what they need) were the two professions
in this space, under the umbrella term **User Centered Design**.

Now the umbrella is **User Experience** (UX), with perhaps a dozen specialties under it — interaction design,
interface design, visual design, content management, and of course usability and IA. The scope changed too:
UCD focused on designing the right product and making sure it was usable; UX claims the user's needs at
**every stage of the life cycle**, from seeing an ad on TV, through purchase and delivery tracking, to
returning the thing at a branch store.

The good news: there's far more awareness now that focusing on the user matters, and it's an easier sell than
it was. The bad news: usability now has a lot of siblings looking for seats at the table, each convinced
their tools are the right ones. The worse news: not many people understand the differences between the
specialties or what each uniquely contributes.

So when someone tells you "I'm in UX", or "usability is so 2002 — it's all UX now", smile graciously and ask:

1. How are you learning about users?
2. How are you testing whether people can use what you're building?
3. How do you get changes to happen?

If they don't do any of those, they need your help. If they do, learn from them.

> It's not what we call ourselves that matters, it's the attitude we bring and the skills we can contribute.

## Two books Recommended for this specific problem

- **Tomer Sharon, *It's Our Research: Getting Stakeholder Buy-In for User Experience Research Projects*.**
  Why: true, pithy, and actionable throughout. Section
  titles include "Become the voice of reason" and "Accept the fact that it might not work and that it's
  okay."
- **Leah Buley, *The User Experience Team of One: A Research and Design Survival Guide*.** Written
  specifically for people who are the only person in their company practising user centered design, or the
  only UX person on their team. Chapters 3 (Building Support for Your Work) and 4 (Growing Yourself and Your
  Career).
