# The line between persuasion and manipulation

Read when a request involves influencing users, persuading them, or making something "more desirable".

## The premise

Usability is, at its heart, a **user advocate** job. Like the Lorax speaking for the trees — except for the
users. It's about serving people better by building better products.

That framing is what makes some requests out of scope, and it's worth being able to state it in one sentence
when a request arrives that doesn't feel right.

## The three-way split

Requests that look similar and are not:

| The request | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Help us make this better for the people using it" | **The job.** | — |
| "Help us influence or persuade users to do X" | **Legitimate — as long as it isn't deceptive.** | Nothing wrong with this. Worth reading: Cialdini's *Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion*, and Susan Weinschenk's books on what neuropsychology teaches about motivation and decision making. The think-aloud protocol in usability tests often produces valuable insight into **why** attempts at persuasion succeed or fail. |
| "Help us make people *think* it's desirable" | **Manipulation. Not part of the job.** | The request isn't for help determining whether something is desirable, or even making it *more* desirable — it's for help making people believe it is. |

The third almost always arrives dressed as the second. This dates the trend to roughly five years before he
wrote, and notes with a stage cough where it usually originates.

## Desirability isn't a usability question anyway

Independent of the ethics, there's a methods problem worth raising first, because it's less confrontational
and often ends the conversation on its own.

You may get a sense during a test session that a participant finds something desirable. **But that's all it
is: a sense.** Whether something is desirable is a market research question, best answered with market
research tools and instruments — as is whether something is *useful*, which should be settled before the
project starts, using interviews, surveys, and focus groups.

Of the seven attributes commonly listed — useful, learnable, memorable, effective, efficient, desirable,
delightful — only **learnable, effective, efficient** sit inside the working definition of usability. So
"can you test whether users find this desirable" has a straightforward, non-moral answer: not with this
instrument.

That answer is often all that's needed. Use it first.

## The gradient

The reason this needs naming in advance is that it's continuous, and each step looks small from the one
before:

**Relatively benign.** A slightly hidden checkbox, checked by default, that automatically signs someone up
for a newsletter.

**Inching toward the dark.** Tricking people into installing an unwanted browser toolbar, and changing their
default search and home page settings while they aren't looking. worked example of the pattern:

1. You click a link to download some free software.
2. A screen opens with three big "Start Download" links.
3. Not noticing the nearly invisible instructions, and with nothing happening, you click one of them.
4. A new page appears with another "Start Download" link, so you click it — and end up downloading software
   you didn't want.

Everyone has been on the receiving end of this.

**Over the line.** Phishing, scamming, identity theft — true black hat.

> Just be aware that if people ask you to do any of this, it's not part of your job. The users are counting
> on you.

## How to decline the third kind

The source material is emphatic a script, but the surrounding material implies the posture, and it matters because a
moralizing refusal loses you the standing to influence the next decision:

- **Lead with the methods answer**, not the ethical one. "A usability test can't measure that" is true,
  useful, and unarguable, and it resolves most of these requests without anyone having to defend their
  motives.
- **Offer the nearest legitimate thing.** If they want people to want the product, the honest versions are:
  make it genuinely better, find out what people actually want with market research, and make the real
  benefit clear and easy to see. Those are all things you can help with.
- **Assume good intent, because it's usually there.** Every usability flaw has a plausible rationale and a
  well-meant intention behind it; the same is generally true of these requests. Someone under revenue
  pressure reaching for a dark pattern is usually not a villain.
- **Say it once, plainly, and move on.** Don't relitigate.

## And on the other side: deliberate depletion can be a real decision

The counterpart worth holding, so this doesn't turn into blanket refusal of anything commercial.

Some user-unfriendly things are legitimate business decisions. Uninvited pop-ups almost always annoy people
to some extent. But if the statistics show ten percent more revenue from using them, and the organization
judges the annoyance worth it, it can do that. **It's a business decision.**

The requirement is only that it be made **in an informed way, rather than inadvertently**. Your job in that
case is to name the cost accurately — how much goodwill it spends and where — not to forbid the choice.

The difference between that and the third column above is deception. Annoying people openly is a trade.
Misleading them isn't.
