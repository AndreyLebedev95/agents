## What a Home page is asked to carry

Concrete demands: site identity and hierarchy, search, content promos (newest, best, most popular), feature
promos, timely content (or at minimum some sign of life — even a recent press release — so it doesn't read
as abandoned), space allocated to advertising and co-branding deals, shortcuts to the most frequently
requested items, and registration/sign-in with a way to show you're signed in.

Abstract demands on top: show me what I'm looking for, expose me to things I'm *not* looking for but might
want, show me where to start, and establish credibility and trust — because for some visitors this is the
only chance the site gets.

And it's all done under pressure. It's the most-seen page, so anything promoted there gets significantly
more traffic, which makes it the waterfront property of the site and the object of fierce turf battles —
especially the space above the fold. It's the one page everybody including the CEO has an opinion about. And
unlike lower-level pages it has to serve every visitor, however diverse.

Given all that, compromise is inevitable on any complex site. The question is what gets lost.

## The Big Bang Theory: the four questions

What gets lost, almost always, is **conveying the big picture**. Whenever is handed a Home page design
to review, the thing he can nearly always count on is that they haven't made it clear enough what the site
*is*.

So the Home page must answer, at a glance, correctly and unambiguously, with very little effort:

1. **What is this?**
2. **What have they got here?**
3. **What can I do here?**
4. **Why should I be here — and not somewhere else?**

Why the first few seconds decide it: a well-known experiment showed people form a set of general impressions
within about 50 milliseconds of opening a page — does it look good, is there a lot of content or a little,
are there clear regions, which ones attract me — and those snap judgments turn out to predict their more
reasoned assessments after spending real time on the page.

The asymmetry that makes this urgent: a *wrong* first assumption is worse than none. People take their first
bits of "knowledge" and use them to interpret everything else they see. If they conclude "this is a site for
X" and they're wrong, they force-fit that reading onto everything they encounter and generate more
misinterpretations. People who start out lost mostly get *loster*.

Everything else on the page matters too — impressing them, enticing them, directing them, exposing them to
deals. But those things don't slip through the cracks: there are always plenty of people inside and outside
the team seeing to it that they get done. Nobody has a vested interest in getting the main point across.

**Test this on outsiders.** You can't trust your own judgment here, because the main point is the one thing
nobody inside the organization will notice is missing.

## Does the Home page still matter?

People are as likely — or more likely — to enter through a link in an email, a blog, or a social post that
lands deep in the site. That's true, and it means every page should do what it can to orient them: who you
are, what you do, what the site offers. But there isn't much room on most pages to do that well.

So a behaviour has emerged: people teleport into the depths, look at the page the link took them to, and
then **go to the Home page to get their bearings** — like divers bobbing up to the surface to see where they
are. If the page was interesting they want to know what else is here; if they need to rely on the
information, they want to know who publishes it and how credible it is. The Home page is still where that
happens.

## Three places people look for the message

Everything on the page contributes to understanding what the site is, but three places carry explicit
statements:

- **The tagline.** The space right next to the Site ID is among the most valuable on the page. A phrase
  visually connected to the ID is read as describing the whole site.
- **The Welcome blurb.** A terse description of the site in a prominent block, usually top-left or centre of
  the content area, where the eye lands first.
- **The "Learn more."** Innovative products and business models need more explanation than most people have
  patience to read — but people are now accustomed to watching short videos and often willing to.

Most users will try to guess what the site is from the overall content first. These three exist so that when
guessing fails, there's somewhere to find out.

Guidelines:

- **Use as much space as necessary.** The temptation is to use none, because you can't imagine anyone not
  knowing what this site is, and because everyone else is clamouring for the space. A genuinely novel
  proposition deserves a lot of the page — Kickstarter's Home page uses almost every element to explain or
  reinforce what it does, with "What is Kickstarter?" as the most prominent item in the primary navigation.
- **...and no more.** For most sites the basic proposition doesn't need much space, and a message occupying
  the whole page is usually too much for anyone to bother absorbing. Just long enough, not longer. Don't
  list every great feature — a few of the most important ones.
- **Never use a mission statement as a Welcome blurb.** "XYZCorp offers world-class solutions in the
  burgeoning field of blah blah blah." Nobody reads them.

## Taglines

A tagline is a pithy phrase characterizing the whole enterprise — what it is and what makes it great. On a
site it sits right below, above, or next to the Site ID. It's an efficient way to get the message across
because it's the one place people most expect to find a concise statement of purpose.

What to look for:

- **Clear and informative** — explains exactly what the site or organization does.
- **Just long enough.** Six to eight words: enough for a full thought, short enough to absorb easily.
- **Conveys differentiation and a clear benefit.** The substitution test: a really good tagline is one that
  **nobody else in the world could use except you**. Apply it literally — if a competitor
  could put it on their site unchanged, rewrite.
- **Personable, lively, sometimes clever.** Clever is good only when the cleverness helps convey the benefit
  rather than obscuring it.
- **Not generic.**

**A tagline is not a motto.** A motto expresses a guiding principle, a goal, or an ideal — lofty and
reassuring. A tagline conveys a value proposition. If someone doesn't know what the thing *is*, a motto won't
tell them. (An organization with a captive audience, like a public utility, can get away with a motto,
because differentiation isn't its problem.)

Some sites can skip it — household-name sites, and sites very well known from their offline origins. argues even those benefit: no matter how well known you are, why pass up an unobtrusive chance to say why
someone is better off here; and a strong offline brand's online mission is never exactly the same, which is
worth explaining.

## The fifth question: where do I start?

Once someone knows what they're looking at, one more question remains. After a quick look around they should
be able to say with confidence:

- Here's where to start if I want to **search**.
- Here's where to start if I want to **browse**.
- Here's where to start if I want to **sample their best stuff**.

On sites built around a step-by-step process — applying for a mortgage, say — the entry point for that
process should leap out. On sites with accounts, register and sign in should be prominent.

The usual thing that obscures these is promotional clutter: it's hard to find the entry points when the page
is full of promos all yelling "Start here!" The fix is to **make entry points look like entry points** — the
search box looking like a search box, the section list looking like a section list — and to label them
literally: *Search*, *Browse by Category*, *Sign in*, *Start here*.

## Promo creep as a commons problem

Home page promotion works *too* well. Anything with a prominent Home page link is virtually guaranteed a
great deal more traffic, which leads every stakeholder to ask why they don't have one.

The structure of the problem is a tragedy of the commons: the promoted section captures the entire gain,
while the loss in overall Home page effectiveness is shared across all sections. So adding one more thing is
individually rational for each stakeholder and collectively fatal — and each stakeholder reasons the same
way, preferably before someone else does.

What to do with this in a review:

- **Name the dynamic explicitly.** Framed as a commons problem it's a resource-allocation question, not a
  taste dispute — which is the only framing in which it can be settled.
- **Establish that the space is a fixed budget**, so additions are trades rather than additions.
- **Offer substitutes**: cross-promotion from other popular pages, and stakeholders taking turns in the same
  slot.
- **Expect it to need constant vigilance.** The degradation is gradual — the slow, inexorable addition of
  just one more thing — which is exactly why nobody notices it happening.
